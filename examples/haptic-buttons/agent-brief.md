# Study brief (given to every agent)

You are forecasting how a group of people performed and felt when they typed number sequences on buttons floating in mid-air in virtual reality, with three kinds of feedback when a button is pressed. You have not seen their answers.

**The setup.** Participants stand wearing a standalone VR headset that tracks their bare hands with its cameras; they hold nothing. A small vibrotactile actuator (a coin-sized vibration motor) is strapped to the pad of the right index finger. Everyone presses with the right index finger, whatever their handedness, because the study has one actuator, built for the right hand. The virtual room is plain and evenly lit.

**The keypad.** A phone-style keypad (digits 1–9, then delete, 0 and an unused blank key) floats at chest height, about 40 cm in front of the participant, tilted slightly towards them. Each key is a 3 cm square with 1 cm gaps. A key activates when the tracked fingertip crosses its **activation plane**, 1 cm behind the key's front face. Nothing physical stops the finger, so it can keep moving past the plane before the person pulls it back. After an activation, the key cannot activate again until the fingertip has moved back in front of its face.

**The task.** A 4-digit sequence (for example 7 3 9 1) appears on a panel above the keypad. The participant types it; the digits typed so far are shown on the panel. A wrong digit is corrected with the delete key and typed again. When all four digits are right, the panel shows the next sequence. Every sequence is new; no sequence repeats within a session.

**The three feedback designs** (the room, the keypad, the activation rule and the task stay the same; only the feedback at a press changes):

- **Visual feedback only:** the key lights up and its face moves back slightly when it activates. No vibration.
- **Vibrotactile buzz:** the same visual feedback, plus one short vibration burst (about 20 ms) at the fingertip at the moment the key activates.
- **Patterned pulse:** the same visual feedback, plus a pattern that follows the press: light ticks begin when the fingertip touches the key's front face, come faster as the finger approaches the activation plane, and end in a distinct, stronger double pulse at activation.

There is no sound in any design.

**The procedure.** 18 adults each use all three designs, one block per design, in a counterbalanced order: each of the six possible orders is used by three people. With each design they:

1. type three practice sequences (not scored), then
2. type ten scored sequences,
3. answer a short questionnaire, and
4. take a short break.

After all three designs they rank them.

**What to predict for each design:**

| Outcome | How it is measured |
| --- | --- |
| Completion time per sequence | Seconds from a sequence appearing to its last correct digit, geometric mean over the ten scored sequences; corrections count as part of the time |
| Error rate | Wrong digit presses (a wrong key, or an unintended second activation of the same key) / all digit presses in the scored sequences, as a proportion; delete presses are not counted |
| Overshoot depth | How far (mm) the fingertip travelled past the activation plane before turning back, mean over all digit presses in the scored sequences |
| Press confidence | "I was confident that each press registered", 1–7 (1 = not at all, 7 = completely) |
| Workload (raw NASA-TLX) | Mean of six items, each 0–100 in steps of 5: mental, physical, temporal, performance, effort, frustration (Performance is scored 0 = perfect, 100 = failure, so every item points the same way) |
| Presence | "I felt the buttons were really there", 1–7 (1 = not at all, 7 = completely) |
| Overall preference | Rank of the three designs at the end of the session, 1 = best, no ties |

Report each value for visual feedback only, vibrotactile buzz and patterned pulse, and for workload the six items, plus your uncertainty and reasoning.
