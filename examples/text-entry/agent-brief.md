# Study brief: mid-air text entry with four virtual keyboards

People type short English phrases in virtual reality with four virtual keyboards. Each person uses all four keyboards, one after another. You have not seen their answers.

**Equipment.** A Meta Quest 3 headset with its two controllers (hand tracking off). Participants sit on a chair without armrests, so their arms are not supported. The virtual room is a plain, evenly lit grey room. Above each keyboard, a panel shows the phrase to type; the text typed so far appears directly below it.

**The four keyboards** (the room, the phrases and the instructions stay the same):

- **Raycast QWERTY (`raycast`).** A flat QWERTY keyboard, about 1 m wide, floats 1.5 m in front of the participant at chest height. Each controller casts a ray; the participant points at a key and pulls the trigger to type it. Either hand can type. Space, Backspace and Enter are keys on the panel. A key lights up and clicks when it is typed.
- **Drum-like keyboard (`drum`).** The QWERTY keys are drum pads on a curved surface in front of the body: 45 cm in front of the chest, 25 cm below shoulder height, tilted 30° towards the participant. A short virtual drumstick (20 cm) extends from each controller, and a key is typed when a stick tip strikes it downwards; the controller vibrates on each hit. The keyboard sits at the same place for everyone and is not adjusted to the person. Space, Backspace and Enter are pads too.
- **Swipe keyboard (`swipe`).** The same flat QWERTY panel as the raycast keyboard, at the same distance. To type a word, the participant holds the trigger and draws the ray through the word's letters in one stroke, then releases. A recogniser with an English dictionary turns the stroke into the closest word and adds a space. The three next-best words appear above the keyboard and can be chosen by pointing at them. A Delete-word key removes the last word. Single letters can also be typed one at a time by pointing and clicking, as on the raycast keyboard.
- **Split keyboard (`split`).** The QWERTY layout is split into two halves, each a small grid of keys floating just above one controller (left half: q–t, a–g, z–b; right half: y–p, h–l, n–m). Each thumbstick moves a highlight across the keys of its half, one key per tilt, and the controller's lower face button (A or X) types the highlighted key; the highlight then returns to the middle of the half. The grip buttons type a space, the upper face buttons (B or Y) are Backspace, and a trigger is Enter. The arms can rest low or in the lap.

No keyboard has word completion or automatic correction, apart from the swipe keyboard's recogniser.

**The phrases.** A pool of 40 short English phrases in the style of the MacKenzie and Soukoreff (2003) phrase set: lowercase letters and spaces only, without capitals, digits or punctuation. They are 18 to 36 characters long (28.7 on average) with 4 to 7 words each, for example "the kettle is on the stove", "please close the door quietly" and "we watched the sunrise from the hill". Each participant gets all 40 phrases in a random order, ten per keyboard, so no phrase is typed twice.

**The instruction** for every phrase: "Type the phrase as quickly and accurately as you can. You may correct mistakes you notice. Press Enter when you are done." Enter submits the phrase as typed, with or without errors, and the next phrase appears. A phrase that is not finished after 180 seconds is ended and the next one appears.

**The procedure.** 24 adults take part, one at a time, in a session of about 60 minutes:

1. Before the headset goes on: consent, a short background questionnaire, a one-minute typing test on a physical desktop keyboard, and a measurement of arm length (shoulder to fingertip).
2. A short introduction to the headset and controllers.
3. Then, for each keyboard, in a counterbalanced order (a balanced 4 × 4 Latin square: four orders, six people each; every keyboard comes once in every position and directly follows every other keyboard once):
   1. a one-minute demonstration by the experimenter, then two minutes of practice with phrases from outside the pool (practice is not scored);
   2. ten scored phrases;
   3. the questionnaire below, answered on a tablet with the headset off;
   4. a two-minute break.
4. After all four keyboards: a ranking of the four keyboards.

**What is measured per participant and keyboard:**

| Measure | How it is measured |
| --- | --- |
| Entry speed (words per minute) | Per phrase (\|T\| − 1) / S × 60 / 5, with T the transcribed phrase and S the seconds from the first key (or first stroke) to Enter; a word counts as five characters. Mean over the ten scored phrases. |
| Uncorrected error rate (proportion, 0–1) | Errors left in the submitted phrase: the minimum string distance between presented and transcribed phrase, divided by the longer of the two. Mistakes corrected before Enter do not count. Mean over the ten scored phrases. |
| Entry time per phrase (seconds) | From the first key (or first stroke) to Enter. Geometric mean over the ten scored phrases. |
| Workload (raw NASA-TLX, 0–100) | Six items after each keyboard, each on a 0–100 scale in steps of 5: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance (0 = perfect, 100 = failure), effort and frustration. The score is the unweighted mean of the six. |
| Arm and shoulder comfort (1–7) | "My arms and shoulders felt comfortable while typing with this keyboard" (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). |
| Preference rank (1–4) | After all four keyboards: "Which keyboard would you choose to enter text in VR?", ranked from 1 (first choice) to 4, without ties. |

Give every measure for every keyboard, with your uncertainty and reasoning.
