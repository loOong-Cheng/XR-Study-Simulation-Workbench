# Background note on tactile feedback for mid-air buttons (invented for this example)

These statements are invented for demonstration. They are not a literature review, they were written without looking at the synthetic participants' answers, and they were not fitted to them. In a real study, put your evidence document here, for example a summary of published findings.

- A physical key stops the finger and clicks. A mid-air key does neither: without something to touch, people judge contact and registration by eye alone.
- With visual feedback alone, the moment of registration is easy to miss when the fingertip hides the key, and people may push deeper or wait for the change of colour before moving on.
- A vibration on the fingertip at the moment of registration adds a cue in a second sense, timed to the event. A cue that marks contact as well as registration tells people where the key surface is.
- Vibration is not pressure: a vibrating ring does not stop the finger, so the key still has no physical surface.
- Questionnaire items about whether an object "felt really there" are sensitive to how objects respond to touch, not only to how they look.
- Mid-air typing holds the arm up without support. Arm fatigue builds over a session and depends on keypad height, distance and how long the task lasts.
- Comfort ratings in head-mounted displays also reflect the headset, the session length and a person's susceptibility to motion sickness, which differ between people independently of the interface.
- People with experience of hand tracking or tactile wearables bring expectations about how virtual objects respond to touch.
