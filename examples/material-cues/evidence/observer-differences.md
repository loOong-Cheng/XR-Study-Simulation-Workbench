# Observer differences in material judgements and VR studies (background note)

*Invented for this example: a literature-style summary of general knowledge about how people differ in material judgements and in VR studies. It is not a review of real papers, it names no results of this study and it was written without its data.*

## Colour vision

- Red–green colour-vision deficiency is common in men and rare in women. Affected observers discriminate hues along the red–green axis poorly, while they see differences in lightness and along the blue–yellow axis much as others do.
- Where two surfaces differ only in a slight red–green tint, deficient observers often cannot tell them apart and fall back on guessing. Observers with normal colour vision can see such a tint, although they still have to learn what it stands for.
- Highlights, reflections and transparency are mostly differences in lightness and sharpness, so cues of that kind are available to deficient observers too.
- Colour-vision status is rarely related to presence or to visual appeal in published VR work.

## Art, design and photography training

- People trained in art, design, photography or 3D graphics attend more to highlights, reflections and surface detail, and describe materials with a more precise vocabulary. In material-naming tasks with realistic images they are often more accurate; the difference shrinks when the images carry few cues.
- Trained viewers may also judge stylised, flat imagery more favourably, as a deliberate aesthetic rather than as a lack of quality.

## Experience with VR

- Experienced VR users point, select and turn with less effort and finish interaction tasks sooner, especially in the early trials of a session.
- First-time users often report a strong sense of presence driven by novelty; with repeated exposure, ratings of presence and wonder decline somewhat.

## Age

- Visual search and pointing slow down across adulthood, and contrast sensitivity declines. Middle-aged adults usually need somewhat more time per trial than young adults in search tasks.

## Vision correction

- Glasses worn inside a headset can press against its lenses, narrow the field of view, slip or fog up; some users report a weaker sense of being in the scene because of this. Contact lenses do not have these problems.

## Order and learning in within-subjects studies

- In a session with two blocks of the same task, the second block is usually completed sooner, whichever version is shown second. Counterbalancing the order spreads this learning evenly over the versions.
